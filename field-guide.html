1 · THE BIG PICTURE

Two files, one Frank

When you press play, index.html loads three scripts in order. The order matters: Frank has to be described before the sketch can make one.

p5 libraryTeaches the browser p5 words like ellipse()
→
frank.jsDescribes Frank: the class
→
sketch.jsMakes Frank and runs the show

setup() runs once

createCanvas(400, 400);
sun = new Sunbeam();
frank = new Frank(190, 360);
lightSlider = createSlider(0, 1023, 300);

Make the canvas, the sun, Frank, and the slider.

draw() runs about 60 times a second

background(255);
sun.strength = lightSlider.value();
sun.display();
frank.update(sun.strength);
frank.display();

Wipe the canvas, set the light, draw the sun, then ask Frank to change his numbers and draw himself.

My note

The draw example on the p5 reference is a mouse click. But I understand alpha and transparency from last week's homework, which I think we're using here.

Clarified Yes. The reference example is just one demo; what matters is that draw() runs over and over. The sunbeam's fill(250, 199, 117, alpha) uses the fourth number for transparency, and map() turns the light strength into that alpha.

My note

frank.update and frank.display come from the Frank file?

Clarified Yes. They're methods written inside the Frank class in frank.js, and sketch.js calls them on its Frank every frame.

2 · CLASS WORDS

The six words that make a class

These are JavaScript, not p5, which is why most of them aren't on the p5 reference page. The p5 reference covers them briefly under Foundation → class, and MDN has a page for each.

WordIn Frank's codeIn plain words
classclass Frank { ... }The recipe for a Frank. Describes what he has and what he can do. Draws nothing by itself.
constructorconstructor(x, y) { ... }Runs once, the moment a Frank is made. Gives him his starting values.
newfrank = new Frank(190, 360)"Make one now." Runs the constructor, with 190 and 360 arriving as x and y.
thisthis.anger = 0"This Frank's own." Anything saved on this sticks around for update() and display() to use later.

methodupdate(), display()A function that belongs to Frank. Something he can do. Called with a dot: frank.display().

Capital vs lowercase. Frank is the class (the recipe in frank.js). frank is the one actual Frank, stored in a variable in sketch.js. Same idea as Creature and blob in the class demo.

My note

Frank with a capital F comes from the frank.js file. We rename him frank with a lowercase f because we're taking a copy from frank.js and giving new attributes to a Frank on this sketch.

Clarified Close. frank isn't a copy with new attributes added. He's one Frank built from the recipe, and he gets exactly the starting properties the constructor gives every Frank. The sketch only hands him 190 and 360, which say where he stands. ("Properties" is the word for his attributes.)

My note

So he's the same Frank, just with a new position.

Clarified Exactly.

My note

Same goes for Sunbeam? And lightSlider is new?

Clarified Yes for Sunbeam: Sunbeam is the class and sun is the one sunbeam. It lives at the bottom of sketch.js, not in frank.js. lightSlider is new, but it isn't a class. createSlider() is a p5 function (on the reference under DOM) that makes a slider, and lightSlider holds it.

3 · HIS NUMBERS

What Frank keeps track of

The constructor sets these up. update() changes some of them each frame. display() only reads them. That split is the main idea of the class: logic in one place, drawing in another.

A class inside a class. Blinker is a second, tiny class at the bottom of frank.js. Frank makes one in his constructor and asks it every frame whether his eyes should be shut. It's the same pattern as Frank himself: constructor, update(), and a method that hands back a number.

4 · PLACING HIM

Two tricks that make the drawing manageable

Turn on his frame in the live Frank to see both.

Move the origin to his feet

push();
translate(this.x, this.y);
// ...draw every part...
pop();

translate() moves the point (0, 0) to the spot between his feet, so every part can be drawn relative to him instead of to the canvas. push() and pop() are brackets: anything changed in between gets undone at pop(), so the sun and the rest of the sketch aren't moved too.

Measure everything in "Franks"

let s = this.size;   // 120
ellipse(s * 0.25, bottom - s * 0.28,
        s * 0.46, s * 0.44);  // belly

Positions are written as fractions of his size. s * 0.46 means "a bit under half a Frank wide." Change this.size to 80 and every part shrinks together. bottom is the bottom of his body, just above his legs.

Breathing and leaning

translate(0, bottom);
rotate(-0.12 * bliss);
scale(1 - 0.012 * this.breath, 1 + 0.022 * this.breath);
translate(0, -bottom);

Hop down to the bottom of his body, tip him back a little when he's content, stretch him slightly taller as he breathes in, then hop back. Doing it from bottom means he stretches upward from his legs instead of from the middle of the canvas.

6 · SCARY BITS, TRANSLATED

JavaScript shortcuts you'll see in his drawing

Each of these looks strange but says something simple. The plain version on the right does the same thing.

Unpacking a list in a loop

for (let [x, y, len] of this.spikes) {
  // use x, y, len
}

"For each spike in the list, call its three numbers x, y, and len." It's a shorter way of writing a regular for (let i = 0; ...) loop with this.spikes[i][0] and so on.

The question-mark if

let start = inward > 0 ? PI + 0.35 : PI - 0.15;

An if/else squeezed into one line: "if inward is more than 0, use the first number, otherwise use the second." It's how the two eyes get mirrored lids.

An arrow function

let lipLineY = (x) => {
  // ...search along the lip curve...
};

A small function saved in a variable, written inside drawMouth() because only the mouth needs it. Give it an x and it hands back where the lip line is, so the tooth sits exactly on the lip.

Vectors

let n = createVector(x, y + 0.55).normalize();

A vector is an arrow: a direction plus a length. This one points from the middle of his body out to the spike, and normalize() sets its length to 1 so it's purely a direction. Only the spikes use this.

Clipping

beginClip();
this.bodyOutline(s, bottom);
endClip();

Works like a stencil. Until the next pop(), everything is only visible inside his body outline, so the belly, scales, and face can't spill past his edges.

Easing with lerp

this.lookX = lerp(this.lookX, goalX, 0.12);

"Move 12% of the way from where you are toward where you want to be." Repeated every frame, it makes things glide instead of jump. His eyes use it now, and his mood will in job 3.

7 · THE MOOD DIAL

One number runs his whole face

anger goes from 1 (annoyed) to -1 (content). Two helpers split it up: annoyed = max(0, anger) only counts the grumpy side, and bliss = max(0, -anger) only counts the happy side. Every feature reads from those.

Featureanger = 1anger = 0anger = -1

MouthDeep frownSmall frownSmall smile

CheeksPlainPlainPink blush
EyesFollow your mouseFollow your mouseGaze up at the sun (below -0.4)

Job 3 in four lines (walkthrough below)

update(light) {
  // dark (0) makes him annoyed, full sun (1023) makes him content
  let targetAnger = map(light, 0, 1023, 0.8, -1);
  // ease toward that a little every frame
  this.anger = lerp(this.anger, targetAnger, 0.03);

Plus frank.update(sun.strength) in sketch.js. That's the code the live Frank above is running.

JOB 3 · FRANK FEELS THE LIGHT

How the light becomes a mood

Two edits connect the sunbeam to Frank. Before job 3 they didn't know about each other: the sun changed, and Frank's anger sat at 0.

lightSliderYou set 0 to 1023
→
sun.strengthThe sun holds it
→
update(light)Frank receives it as light
→
this.angerHis mood moves toward it

Edit 1, in sketch.js: hand it over

frank.update(sun.strength);

The value inside the parentheses is passed to Frank. He doesn't know or care whether it came from a slider or an Arduino. He just gets a light level.

Edit 2, in frank.js: catch it

update(light) {
  let targetAnger = map(light, 0, 1023, 0.8, -1);
  this.anger = lerp(this.anger, targetAnger, 0.03);
  // ...blinking, breathing, eyes as before

light in the parentheses is a parameter: a name for whatever value gets handed in. Same idea as x and y in the constructor.

Step 1: map() picks the mood he should be in

map(light, 0, 1023, 0.8, -1) reads as: "light goes from 0 to 1023, so stretch it onto a range from 0.8 to -1." The order flips on purpose, so more light means less anger.

LightTarget angerHow he looks
0 (dark)0.80Grumpy
3000.27Mildly put out
512 (halfway)-0.10Indifferent
800-0.61Content, gazing at the sun
1023 (full sun)-1.00As happy as Frank gets

Why 0.8 and not 1? In total darkness he's grumpy, not furious. Change it to 1 if you want a crankier Frank.

My note

map makes sense! The 800 is good too, so you don't have to go all the way over on the slider.

Step 2: lerp() eases him there

If his anger jumped straight to the target, his face would snap. Instead, every frame he moves 3% of the remaining distance. Here's the slider jumping from dark to full sun, with his anger starting at 0:

FrameTimeanger
00 s0.00
10.02 s-0.03
20.03 s-0.06
300.5 s-0.60
601 s-0.84
1202 s-0.97

Big steps at first, smaller as he gets close. That's why he seems to warm up and then settle.

Try it: his personality is one number

The 0.03 is how quickly he changes his mind. Try 0.2 and he reacts almost instantly, like an eager pet. Try 0.005 and he takes ages to come around, which is very Frank.

My note

lerp! This is great. I hate when I see snapping animation, so this is a good one to remember. I changed it to 0.5 to really see the difference, then kept 0.03.

Clarified At 0.5 he covers half the remaining distance every frame, so he gets there in a few frames, close to a snap.

My note

I wonder how well the photoresistor works when we get there.

Clarified Good instinct. Real sensors rarely use the full 0 to 1023, so in job 4 we log its actual darkest and brightest readings, then use those in map().

JOB 4 · LAB DAY

Wire Frank's light sensor

Goal for the lab: the Arduino reads the photoresistor and prints numbers you can see change when you cover it or shine a light on it. No p5 yet. That's job 5.

Bring

Arduino Uno R3 and its USB cable, a breadboard, the photoresistor, a 10k resistor (brown, black, orange bands), and five jumper wires: two red, two black, one yellow. Colors don't change how it works, they just keep it readable.

How it works

The photoresistor and the 10k resistor share column 13, and A0 listens to that spot. More light lets more of the 5V through the photoresistor, so A0 reads a higher number. Dark means a low number. That matches Frank: high light, happy Frank.

Rings mark where a wire or leg goes into a hole. A wire only connects where it ends, not where it crosses a rail. Highlighted columns are the three that matter: 10, 13 and 18.

Wire or partFromTo
RedArduino 5VBottom + rail
BlackArduino GNDBottom − rail
Red jumperBottom + railj10
Photoresistorg10g13 (either way round)
10k resistorh13h18 (either way round)
Black jumperj18Bottom − rail
Yellowi13Arduino A0

The Arduino code

// Frank's light sensor: reads the photoresistor and sends the number to the computer
const int sensorPin = A0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);                  // open the line to the computer
}

void loop() {
  int light = analogRead(sensorPin);   // 0 to 1023
  Serial.println(light);               // send it, one number per line
  delay(50);                           // about 20 readings a second
}

Same tools as your class work: analogRead to read, Serial.println to send. Type it in yourself and save it as frank_light in the Arduino IDE. In job 5, p5 will listen to exactly these numbers.

In the lab, step by step

  1. Wire it with the USB unplugged. Follow the table row by row, checking off each one.

  2. Plug in and upload. In the Arduino IDE, pick the board (Arduino Uno) and its port, then upload frank_light.

  3. Open the Serial Monitor (magnifying glass, top right) and set it to 9600 baud. Numbers should scroll.

  4. Test it. Cover the photoresistor with your hand: numbers drop. Shine your phone flashlight on it: numbers climb. For a picture of it, try the Serial Plotter next to it.

  5. Log your readings in the table below. These go into map() in job 5 so Frank uses the sensor's real range.

  6. Close the Serial Monitor and Plotter when you're done. Only one program can listen to the Arduino at a time, and in job 5 that will be p5.

The Arduino is already sending one number per line. Job 5 teaches the sketch to listen, using p5.webserial, a library on p5's libraries page that works in the web editor in Chrome. If no Arduino is connected, the slider still works, so anyone can play with Frank.

The heart of it, in draw()

if (port.opened()) {
  let reading = port.readUntil("\n");
  if (reading.length > 0) {
    sun.strength = int(reading);
  }
} else {
  sun.strength = lightSlider.value();
}

Connected: use the sensor, but only when a full number has arrived, so the sun never gets an empty value. Not connected: use the slider, like before.

My note

I tried adding connectArduino() on my own and pasted it between function draw() and its {, which broke draw(). "After draw()" was confusing: after the line, or after the whole function?

Clarified After the whole function. Every function is its name, then {, its code, then }. A new function starts only after the closing } of the one before it, never inside it.

Using the lab readings

map(light, 100, 800, 0.8, -1, true)

Every 0 and 1023 becomes 100 and 800, the sensor's real range: in Frank's mood, the sunbeam's alpha, and the slider. The extra true is on the map() reference: it keeps the answer inside the range, so a reading of 50 or 900 can't push Frank past fully grumpy or fully content.

Connecting, step by step

  1. Close the Arduino Serial Monitor and Plotter. Only one program can listen at a time.

  2. Use Chrome (or Edge). Safari and Firefox don't support Web Serial.

  3. Press play, then click Connect Arduino under the canvas.

  4. Pick the Arduino in Chrome's pop-up (it usually says Arduino Uno, or usbmodem on a Mac) and click Connect.

  5. Cover the sensor, then shine a light on it. Frank should sulk, then come around.

8 · IN CLASS

Things you can say about him

  • Frank is a class. frank.js describes him, and sketch.js makes one with new and asks him to update and draw himself every frame.
  • His mood is one number, anger, from 1 to -1. His brows, lids, mouth, posture, and blush all read from it.
  • The light sets a target mood, and lerp eases him toward it, so he warms up slowly instead of snapping.
  • He's drawn around a point between his feet, in fractions of his size, so moving or resizing him only takes changing a couple of numbers.
  • His look carries over from my Frank & Frazzle sketch, which I built exploring with Claude. This week's work is pulling him out into his own class and giving him a sensor to react to.
